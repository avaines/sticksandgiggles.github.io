This piece started as an offcut from my mate Colin's new kitchen before making its way to my garage. I don't know where the timber came from before that, but the wider parts of this bowl are now a worktop in a Leeds flat. The wood itself is African teak, it's a beautiful wood that finishes really nicely.

Here is how the bowl went from kitchen offcut to the finished thing.

<figure class="process-step">
  <img src="/journal/20260729_bowl_start_to_finish/01-edges-off.jpeg" alt="The corners being cut from the African teak blank on the bandsaw" loading="lazy">
  <figcaption><strong>1. Roughing out the blank.</strong> I started on the bandsaw, knocking off the corners until the timber was roughly circular. You can turn it as a wonky shape but it tends to make the lathe wobble and it'll dull my lathe tools faster.</figcaption>
</figure>

<figure class="process-step">
  <img src="/journal/20260729_bowl_start_to_finish/02-find-the-centre.jpeg" alt="Lines drawn across the timber blank to find its centre" loading="lazy">
  <figcaption><strong>2. Finding the centre.</strong> A few lines across the blank gave me a good enough centre point for mounting it.</figcaption>
</figure>

<figure class="process-step">
  <img src="/journal/20260729_bowl_start_to_finish/03-faceplate-and-mount.jpeg" alt="The African teak blank mounted on the lathe using a faceplate" loading="lazy">
  <figcaption><strong>3. On to the lathe.</strong> I fitted a faceplate rather than just using a spur drive and turning between centres, which I have never really got on with as it doesn't quite feel as secure.</figcaption>
</figure>

<figure class="process-step">
  <img src="/journal/20260729_bowl_start_to_finish/04-foot.jpeg" alt="A mortise being turned into the foot of the bowl" loading="lazy">
  <figcaption><strong>4. Adding a foot.</strong> I prefer a mortise to a tenon for holding a piece like this. I feel as though it wastes less material. It doesn't, but I feel happier, and it is my bowl.</figcaption>
</figure>

<figure class="process-step">
  <img src="/journal/20260729_bowl_start_to_finish/05-shape-and-sand.jpeg" alt="The outside of the bowl shaped and sanded on the lathe" loading="lazy">
  <figcaption><strong>5. Shaping the outside.</strong> With the exterior profile where I wanted it, I sanded through the grits to 600 or 800 and burnished the surface with the shavings giving a lovely shine.</figcaption>
</figure>

<figure class="process-step">
  <img src="/journal/20260729_bowl_start_to_finish/06-hog-it-out.jpeg" alt="The middle of the bowl being hollowed on the lathe" loading="lazy">
  <figcaption><strong>6. Hollowing it out.</strong> I flipped the piece into the chuck and started hogging out the middle. This is my favourite bit of turning; my mind just sort of goes on standby and its incredibly relaxing.</figcaption>
</figure>

<figure class="process-step">
  <img src="/journal/20260729_bowl_start_to_finish/07-pencil-marks.jpeg" alt="Pencil marks covering the inside of the bowl before hand sanding" loading="lazy">
  <figcaption><strong>7. The pencil test.</strong> I have never quite got the hang of power sanding the inside of a smaller bowl and always end up with tool marks all over the piece. Instead, I scribble over it with a pencil and sand the marks away bit by bit. When the pencil has gone, I know I have sanded it consistently.</figcaption>
</figure>

<figure class="process-step">
  <img src="/journal/20260729_bowl_start_to_finish/08-burnished.jpeg" alt="The smoothly sanded and burnished inside of the bowl" loading="lazy">
  <figcaption><strong>8. Sanding and burnishing.</strong> The inside also went to about 600 or 800 grit before another burnish with the shavings.</figcaption>
</figure>

<figure class="process-step">
  <img src="/journal/20260729_bowl_start_to_finish/09-foot-off.jpeg" alt="The bowl reverse mounted so the foot can be finished" loading="lazy">
  <figcaption><strong>9. Finishing the bottom.</strong> I flipped it over once more and took off the foot. I made a little adapter for the live centre from hot-glue a while back to give me a nice friction surface which doesn't mark the piece.
</figcaption>
</figure>

Someone once told me that the first thing people do when they pick up your bowl will be to feel the base. Because of this, I usually take the base one grit higher than the rest of the bowl.

<figure class="process-step process-step--finished">
  <img src="/journal/20260729_bowl_start_to_finish/10-finished.jpeg" alt="The finished African teak bowl, oiled and photographed in a light box" loading="lazy">
  <figcaption><strong>10. Finished.</strong>Finally a trip to the AliExpress light box. About 90 minutes from start to finish, with teak oil to bring out the grain.</figcaption>
</figure>
